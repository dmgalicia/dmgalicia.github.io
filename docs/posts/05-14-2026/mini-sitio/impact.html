When cyberattacks leave the screen

Cyberattacks do not end inside a computer system. When they reach critical infrastructure, such as hospitals, banks, public agencies, transport networks, or energy services, their effects move into everyday life. These systems support essential functions in daily society [1]. A digital incident can become a delayed medical service, an interrupted payment system, a government platform taken offline, or a recovery process that costs millions.

This section examines the impact side of cybercrime. The analysis focuses on reported incidents affecting critical infrastructure, economic losses linked to different forms of cybercrime, and ransomware indicators across strategic sectors. Because impact data is less consistently reported than attack counts, the figures move between reported regional aggregates, available country samples, and sector level indicators. They should be read as a focused view of documented consequences rather than a complete measurement of all cyber damage in Latin America.

Critical infrastructure under pressure

FIG. 5 shows how reported cyber incidents affecting critical infrastructure increased between 2020 and 2023 in the available country sample. The pattern is not limited to one sector. Every category moves upward, suggesting that essential services became more exposed as digital dependence expanded.

Hospitals concentrate the largest number of incidents and show the sharpest growth, especially toward the end of the period. This makes healthcare one of the most sensitive points in the sample’s digital infrastructure. When hospital systems fail, the consequences can move quickly from technical disruption to human risk.

Other sectors grow more moderately but remain important. Telecoms, Financial Infrastructure, and Energy show steady increases, while Transportation and Water Systems stay at lower levels. The hierarchy matters because it shows that cyber risk is not only about how many systems are attacked. It is also about which services society depends on most.

Cybercrime becomes an economic shock

FIG. 6 translates cybercrime into financial terms using the reported regional aggregate available in the dataset. The visualization shows that losses are not evenly distributed across categories. Some forms of cybercrime generate much larger costs than others. Financial Fraud dominates the chart and represents the largest share of reported losses. Its weight reflects how cybercrime often follows financial opportunity, especially where digital payments, banking platforms, and online transactions create high value targets.

Ransomware also becomes increasingly important, especially after 2022. Its cost is not limited to the ransom itself. It includes operational paralysis, system recovery, lost productivity, and long-term reputational damage. Smaller categories such as Intellectual Property, Downtime Costs, and Reputational Damage remain below the dominant losses, but they show that the economic impact of cybercrime extends beyond immediate theft.

Ransomware and the cost of interruption

Ransomware is one of the clearest examples of how a cyberattack becomes a social and economic disruption. It does not simply enter a system. It stops work, blocks access, delays services, and forces organizations to recover under pressure. Here, the analysis shifts from country coverage to sector level impact. FIG. 7 shows that downtime worsened across every sector in the dataset. Increase markers are consistently above the Baseline, meaning that recovery times became longer between 2020 and 2023.

The most critical case is healthcare, where downtime rises to more than a month by the end of the period. Government follows with a shorter but still severe interruption window. This difference matters because downtime is not only a technical metric. In hospitals and public services, every additional day offline can affect access, continuity, and trust.

The price attackers think a sector can pay

FIG. 8 keeps the analysis at the sector level and shows that ransom demands are not distributed equally. The financial sector faces the highest average payments, reaching close to several million dollars by 2023, followed by manufacturing. This pattern reflects a basic logic of ransomware. Attackers adjust their demands according to the perceived value of the target and its ability to pay.

Education and government show lower ransom levels, but that does not mean they are safe. Instead, the chart suggests that ransomware groups are becoming more strategic. They do not only attack systems. They estimate how much pressure an organization can absorb and how much money it may be willing to pay to restore operations.

The damage continues after systems return

FIG. 9 keeps the sector level focus and shifts the analysis from interruption to extraction. Ransomware and related attacks do not only block access to systems. They also expose information that can remain valuable long after the initial incident.

Government records the highest volume of stolen data, approaching ninety million records by 2023. Healthcare follows with another steep increase, reflecting the value of medical, personal, fiscal, and institutional information. These two sectors stand out because they manage data that is not only sensitive, but also difficult for citizens to replace or control once exposed.

Together, the three ransomware indicators show a broader pattern. The cost of cybercrime is measured not only in payments, but in downtime, data loss, institutional trust, and the long recovery that follows an attack.
