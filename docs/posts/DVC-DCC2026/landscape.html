Defenses under pressure

Cybersecurity is not only a matter of attacks. It is also a matter of preparation. A country can improve its legal, technical, and institutional defenses while still becoming a larger target for cybercrime. In Latin America, resilience is therefore shaped by a difficult balance. Cyber resilience can be read as part of a broader risk management cycle that includes governance, identification, protection, detection, response, and recovery [1].

This section examines that balance through four dimensions. Institutional readiness, legal milestones, criminal penalties, and cybersecurity workforce capacity. The figures do not all use the same coverage. Some focus on the five country core used throughout the project, while the workforce chart narrows the view to countries with available labor market data. Together, they show that regional resilience is not built all at once. It emerges through laws, institutions, reporting systems, professional skills, and the ability to keep pace with a threat landscape that grows faster every year.

Prepared, but still exposed

FIG. 10 compares two realities within the five country core. The level of institutional preparedness, measured through the Global Cybersecurity Index, and the total volume of reported cyberattacks. The GCI is used here as a reference for country level cybersecurity commitment across legal, technical, organizational, capacity development, and cooperation dimensions [2].

Mexico reaches the highest GCI score in the sample and shows clear progress in technical and legal readiness. Yet that preparation is challenged by a high and still growing volume of attacks. Brazil also improves its cybersecurity score, but remains the largest target in the group.

This creates an uneven endurance race. Countries with larger economies and deeper digital activity become more attractive to cybercriminals, even as they strengthen their defenses. In that sense, cyber resilience is not a fixed position. It is the ability to keep improving while the pressure continues to rise.

Argentina appears with the lowest GCI score among the five countries, while still facing a persistent volume of attacks. Chile occupies a more favorable position, combining a high readiness score with a comparatively lower attack volume. The contrast suggests that resilience depends not only on preparation, but also on how attractive each country is as a target.

Laws as the first layer of defense

FIG. 11 shows how the five country sample has built cybersecurity defenses over time. The timeline follows four milestones. CERT creation, Cybercrime Law, Data Protection Law, and National Strategy.

The sequence matters because cybersecurity capacity is cumulative. A country first needs institutions that can respond to incidents, laws that define digital crime, rules that protect data, and strategies that coordinate national action. These milestones do not eliminate cyber risk, but they create the basic architecture for managing it.

Argentina appears as an early mover in data protection, while Chile leads the technical response timeline with an early CERT. Between the late 2000s and the mid 2010s, the five country sample begins to formalize cybercrime laws and national strategies. The broader message is clear. Latin America has been building its defenses gradually, but not at the same pace in every country.

When penalties change the risk equation

FIG. 12 keeps the focus on the same five country sample and shows that legal readiness is not only about having laws. It is also about how strongly cybercrime is punished and whether incidents must be reported. In the digital world, uneven penalties can create uneven incentives. Some countries may appear less risky for attackers than others.

Mexico has the highest maximum prison sentence in the sample, but it does not require mandatory incident reporting. This creates a gap between punishment and visibility. A country can impose strong penalties while still allowing many incidents to remain outside the public record.

Chile combines a high maximum penalty with Mandatory reporting, making its legal framework more visible from an accountability perspective. Brazil and Colombia sit near the sample median and also require reporting. Argentina shows the lowest maximum penalty among the five countries and does not require mandatory reporting.

The key point is that the legal border can change the cost of risk. If penalties, reporting rules, and enforcement capacity differ widely, attackers may adapt their behavior to those gaps.

The missing workforce behind cyber resilience

FIG. 13 shifts the focus from laws to people. Unlike the previous charts, this visualization narrows the view to countries with available cybersecurity labor market data. Cyber resilience depends not only on institutions and penalties, but also on whether countries have enough qualified professionals to defend systems, investigate incidents, and sustain cybersecurity operations.

Brazil has the strongest labor market in the chart, combining the largest number of cybersecurity jobs with the highest average salary. Mexico also shows strong demand, while Chile remains competitive through relatively high salaries despite a smaller market. Colombia has a meaningful number of openings, but lower salary levels compared with the rest of the group.

The size of the circles adds another layer. Across countries and years, only about half of available positions are filled. This suggests that the workforce gap is not simply caused by a lack of jobs. It points to a shortage of qualified professionals with the skills needed to occupy those roles.

Taken together, this section shows that regional resilience is uneven. Some countries are better prepared, some are more exposed, some have stronger legal tools, and some have deeper labor markets. The challenge for Latin America is to make these defenses grow together, while recognizing that not every indicator is documented with the same country coverage.
